Reading short messages and notices

Find who wrote a message, why, and what you need to do, in emails, texts and signs.

Download PDF

Short messages are everywhere: texts from friends, work emails, notices in buildings. For each one, ask three questions: Who wrote it? Why? What must I do?

PurposeTypical language
InvitingWould you like to... ? Do you fancy... ? Are you free on...?
Asking for somethingCould you... ? Can you remember to...?
Changing a planI'm afraid... Instead of... It's been moved to...
Warning or rulePlease do not... ... must not... ... is not allowed.

Texts

A. Hi Sara, I'm afraid I can't make lunch tomorrow, my boss has called a meeting. Are you free on Thursday instead? Same place, 1 pm? Nora

B. NOTICE: The lift will be closed for repairs from Monday 9 to Wednesday 11 June. Please use the stairs. We are sorry for any trouble.

C. Dear all, Please remember to send me your holiday dates by Friday so I can plan the summer timetable. Thanks, Ahmed (Office Manager)

Look for the verb that asks you to act. Please use, remember to send, are you free: these words show what the reader must do.
Common mistake. Choosing an answer because it has a word from the text. Message A mentions meeting, but the message is about changing lunch plans.

Practice

  1. Message A: Why is Nora writing?
  2. Message A: What day does she suggest?
  3. Message B: How long will the lift be closed?
  4. Message B: What should people do?
  5. Message C: Who is Ahmed writing to?
  6. Message C: What must they do, and by when?
Show answers
  1. To cancel lunch tomorrow and suggest another day.
  2. Thursday
  3. Three days (Monday 9 to Wednesday 11 June)
  4. Use the stairs.
  5. Everyone in the office (all staff)
  6. Send their holiday dates by Friday.
