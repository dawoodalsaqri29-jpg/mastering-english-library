Must, have to and should

Talk about rules, obligations and advice, and know when something is not necessary.

Download PDF

English uses different modal verbs for rules, personal obligations and advice. The hardest part is the negative: mustn't and don't have to mean very different things.

MeaningFormExample
Strong obligation (often the speaker's rule)must + verbI must call my mum tonight.
Obligation from outside (rules, jobs)have to + verbWe have to wear a uniform at work.
Prohibition: it is not allowedmustn't + verbYou mustn't smoke in the hospital.
No obligation: it is not necessarydon't have to + verbYou don't have to bring food. There's plenty.
Adviceshould / shouldn't + verbYou should see a doctor. You shouldn't stay up so late.
Past obligationhad to + verbI had to wait an hour for the bus.
Only one past form. Must has no past tense, so use had to: Yesterday I had to work late, not I must worked late.
Common mistakes. You must to go is wrong: no to after must or should. And You mustn't pay means paying is forbidden; if paying is simply optional, say You don't have to pay.

Practice

  1. Complete: Tomorrow is a holiday, so I ___ get up early. (no obligation)
  2. Complete: You ___ use your phone during the exam. (not allowed)
  3. Correct the mistake: She must to finish the report today.
  4. Give advice: My friend has a bad headache.
  5. Put into the past: I have to show my passport.
Show answers
  1. don't have to
  2. mustn't
  3. She must finish the report today.
  4. For example: You should take a break and drink some water.
  5. I had to show my passport.
