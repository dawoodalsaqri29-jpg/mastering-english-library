Idioms and nuance

Idiomatic expressions with subtle meanings that mark near-native fluency.

Download PDF

At C2 the challenge is not knowing more words but choosing the one with exactly the right shade of meaning. These idioms are frequent in journalism and conversation.

  • a double-edged swordidiom

    something with both good and bad effects

    Fame can be a double-edged sword.

  • to split hairsidiom

    to argue about tiny, unimportant differences

    Let's not split hairs over the wording.

  • to toe the lineidiom

    to follow the rules or official position

    Ministers are expected to toe the party line.

  • a moot pointidiom

    something open to debate, or no longer relevant

    Whether he knew is a moot point now.

  • to pay lip service toidiom

    to say you support something without acting on it

    The company only pays lip service to diversity.

  • the elephant in the roomidiom

    an obvious problem nobody mentions

    The budget cut was the elephant in the room.

  • to have a field dayidiom

    to enjoy an opportunity, often to criticise

    The press had a field day with the scandal.

  • to beg the questionidiom

    to make someone want to ask; also, to assume the conclusion in an argument

    This begs the question of who will pay.

  • by the same tokenphrase

    for the same reason

    He's honest, and by the same token, rather blunt.

  • to throw caution to the windidiom

    to act without worrying about risks

    She threw caution to the wind and quit her job.
