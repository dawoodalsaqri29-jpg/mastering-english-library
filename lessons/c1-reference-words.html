Following an argument: reference words

Track what this, which, such, the former and the latter point back to, so long texts stay clear.

Download PDF

Academic writers avoid repeating nouns by using reference words. If you lose track of what a word refers to, you lose the argument. Train yourself to find the referent every time.

Reference wordUsually refers toExample
this / that / theseAn idea or noun just mentionedPrices rose sharply. This led to protests.
which (after a comma)The noun just before it, or the whole previous clause (as here)Sales fell, which worried investors.
such + nounThings of the type just describedSuch measures are rarely popular.
the former / the latterThe first / second of two thingsSolar and wind power are growing; the latter is cheaper.
it / they / themThe nearest suitable nounThe reports were late because they needed checking.

Text

Many cities have introduced congestion charges or car-free zones. The former raise money that can be spent on public transport, while the latter make streets safer for pedestrians. Critics argue that such schemes hurt small shops, which depend on customers who drive. This concern, however, is not supported by recent studies, many of which show that footfall actually increases once traffic is reduced.

Ask "this what?" When you see a bare this, add a noun in your head: this concern, this change. Good writers often add it for you.
Common mistake. Assuming it or they always refers to the subject of the last sentence. Check meaning: Critics argue that such schemes hurt small shops, which depend on customers: which refers to the shops, not the schemes.
