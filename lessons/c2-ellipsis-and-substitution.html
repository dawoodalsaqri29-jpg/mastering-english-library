Ellipsis and substitution

Avoid repetition the way fluent speakers do, by leaving words out or replacing them with so, do, one and not.

Download PDF

Native speakers rarely repeat whole phrases. They leave out words that are clear from context (ellipsis) or replace them with short words (substitution). Using these well makes your English sound natural and concise.

DeviceReplacesExample
one / onesa countable nounI need a pen. Have you got a spare one?
do / do soa verb phraseShe said she'd apologise, and she did so the next day.
so / nota whole clauseWill it rain? I hope not. Is he coming? I think so.
Ellipsis after auxiliariesthe rest of the verb phraseI haven't read it, but Sara has.
Ellipsis after tothe infinitive phraseI didn't want to go, but I had to.
Ellipsis in coordinationa repeated subject or verbSome ordered fish, others chicken.
Mind the auxiliary. The auxiliary must match the verb you leave out: He can swim, and so can she; They've finished, but we haven't.
Common mistakes. I think yes and I hope no are not English: say I think so and I hope not. Also, one cannot replace an uncountable noun: I prefer hot water to cold, not to cold one.

Practice

  1. Shorten: I haven't seen the film, but my brother has seen the film.
  2. Reply with substitution: "Is the shop open on Fridays?" (you believe it is)
  3. Correct the mistake: "Will we be late?" "I hope no."
  4. Replace the repeated noun: I don't like the blue shirt; I prefer the red shirt.
  5. Shorten: She wanted to leave early, but she wasn't allowed to leave early.
Show answers
  1. I haven't seen the film, but my brother has.
  2. I think so.
  3. I hope not.
  4. I don't like the blue shirt; I prefer the red one.
  5. She wanted to leave early, but she wasn't allowed to.
