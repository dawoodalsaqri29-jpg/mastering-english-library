Giving feedback diplomatically

Praise, criticise and suggest improvements to colleagues or classmates in a way that is honest but tactful.

Download PDF

Giving feedback is one of the hardest things to do well in a second language. Too direct sounds rude; too soft and the message is lost. C1 speakers use a clear structure and softening language to keep both the relationship and the message.

StepLanguage
Start with something specific and positiveI really liked the way you opened with a question.
Introduce the issue gentlyOne thing you might want to look at is... I wonder if...
Describe the effect, not the personThe middle section felt a little long, so some people lost focus.
Suggest, don't orderPerhaps you could... It might be worth trying...
Check and encourageDoes that make sense? Overall, it's a really strong start.
Use softeners. A bit, slightly, a little, not quite and the past continuous (I was wondering whether...) reduce the force of criticism without changing its meaning.
Common mistake. Using but immediately after praise: Great work, but... makes listeners ignore the praise. Try and or a full stop: Great work. One thing to build on is...

Practice

A colleague has just given a presentation. Make each piece of feedback more diplomatic.

  1. Your slides had too much text.
  2. You spoke too fast.
  3. Start with specific praise about their examples.
  4. Suggest, don't order: Add a summary at the end.
  5. Finish the feedback encouragingly.
Show answers
  1. Some of the slides were a little text-heavy, so it was hard to read and listen at the same time.
  2. At times the pace was slightly fast, so a few key points were easy to miss.
  3. The real examples you used made the data much easier to understand.
  4. It might be worth adding a short summary at the end.
  5. Overall, it was a really clear presentation, and these are just small things to polish.
