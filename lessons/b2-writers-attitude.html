Understanding the writer's attitude

Spot the words that show what a writer really thinks, even when they never say "I think".

Download PDF

Writers often show their opinion through word choice rather than by stating it. Exam questions such as What is the writer's attitude to...? test whether you notice these signals.

SignalExamplesWhat it shows
Evaluative adjectivesa promising idea, a costly mistakePositive or negative judgement
Attitude adverbssurprisingly, unfortunately, admittedlyThe writer's reaction to a fact
Reporting verbsclaims, insists, points out, admitsWhether the writer trusts the source
Contrast wordshowever, yet, even soWhere the writer's real point usually comes

Text

The city council claims that the new bike lanes have reduced traffic. Admittedly, there are more cyclists on the main road than last year. Yet the council has published no figures, and the lanes stop suddenly at the busiest junctions, which is hardly a recipe for safety. It is a promising start, but it is far from the transport revolution we were promised.

Reporting verbs carry opinion. Points out means the writer agrees with the source. Claims often means the writer has doubts.
Common mistake. Choosing an answer because its words appear in the text. The text says promising, but the writer's overall attitude is critical: everything after Yet matters most.

Practice

  1. Which word shows the writer doubts the council?
  2. What does Admittedly tell you about the writer?
  3. What is the effect of hardly a recipe for safety?
  4. Overall, the writer's attitude to the bike lanes is: (a) enthusiastic (b) neutral (c) cautiously critical
  5. Rewrite this neutral sentence to sound critical: The report says sales went up.
Show answers
  1. claims
  2. The writer accepts one point against their own view before arguing back.
  3. It is understatement: the writer means the lanes are unsafe.
  4. (c) cautiously critical
  5. For example: The report claims that sales went up. / The report insists, without evidence, that sales went up.
