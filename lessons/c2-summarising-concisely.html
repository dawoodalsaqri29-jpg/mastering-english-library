Summarising a text concisely

Reduce a long text to its essential argument in your own words, keeping the writer's stance without adding your own.

Download PDF

A good summary is short, accurate and neutral. It captures the main argument and the key support, uses your own words, and makes clear that the ideas belong to the original writer. This skill is central to academic and professional writing.

StepWhat to doLanguage
1. Identify the thesisFind the writer's central claimThe author argues / contends / maintains that...
2. Select key pointsKeep reasons; drop examples and detailThis is attributed to... The writer cites...
3. ParaphraseChange structure and vocabulary, not meaning"Costs have soared" → expenses have risen sharply
4. CombineJoin ideas with precise linkersWhile acknowledging..., the author concludes that...
5. CheckRemove your own opinion; verify accuracyUse reporting verbs that reflect the writer's certainty.
Choose the reporting verb carefully. Suggests is tentative, argues is neutral, insists and dismisses are strong. The verb itself tells the reader how confident or critical the writer is.
Common mistakes. Copying phrases from the original, including examples and statistics that are not essential, or adding I think. A summary reports; it does not evaluate.

Practice

Original: "Although electric cars are often presented as the solution to urban pollution, they still produce particles from tyres and brakes, and they do nothing to reduce congestion. Cities would gain more from investing in public transport and cycling."

  1. What is the writer's central claim?
  2. Paraphrase: "they do nothing to reduce congestion".
  3. Choose the best reporting verb: The writer (suggests / insists / argues) that public transport is a better investment.
  4. What is wrong with this summary: "I agree that electric cars are not the answer"?
  5. Write a one-sentence summary.
Show answers
  1. Public transport and cycling are a better answer to urban problems than electric cars.
  2. they leave traffic levels unchanged
  3. argues (the claim is made firmly but without extreme language)
  4. It gives the summariser's opinion instead of reporting the writer's view.
  5. The writer argues that, since electric cars still pollute and leave traffic levels unchanged, cities should invest in public transport and cycling instead.
