How many times a day do you say, "I think"?

I think about it. I think so. I think he's right. It's a useful word, but if you use it in every sentence, your English starts to sound flat.

Today, you're going to learn eight precise verbs that native speakers use instead of "think".

Ponder, contemplate, deliberate, ruminate. And presume, speculate, surmise, envisage.

We'll learn what each one means, hear them in real life, fix the most common mistakes, practise the pronunciation, and finish with a quiz. Let's begin.

Here's the big idea. "Think" can mean many different things, and each meaning has a sharper word.

The first group is about thinking carefully. Ponder, contemplate, deliberate, and ruminate.

The second group is about guessing and imagining. Presume, speculate, surmise, and envisage.

So instead of "I'm thinking about it", you can say "I'm pondering it". Instead of "I think it's true", you can say "I presume it's true". Same idea, but much more exact.

Ponder.

It means to think carefully about something for a long time.

For example: She pondered the offer for a week before she said yes.

Useful collocations: Ponder a question. Ponder the meaning of something. Pause to ponder.

Contemplate.

It means to think seriously about doing something in the future.

For example: He is contemplating a move to Muscat.

Useful collocations: Contemplate leaving. Seriously contemplate. Contemplate the possibility.

Deliberate.

As a verb, it means to think or talk carefully before a decision, often in a group.

For example: The jury deliberated for six hours.

Useful collocations: Deliberate on, or over, something. Deliberate for hours. After much deliberation.

Ruminate.

It means to think about the same thing again and again, usually something that worries you.

For example: Don't ruminate on one bad mark. Learn from it and move on.

Useful collocations: Ruminate on. Ruminate over the past. Endlessly ruminate.
