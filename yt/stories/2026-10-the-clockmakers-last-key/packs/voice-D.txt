Chapter four. The Hour He Did Not Turn.

On Friday evening, the square was full of music, roasted chestnuts and the warm glow of a hundred candles.

Elias carried the glass globe in both arms, wrapped in a blanket, one slow step at a time.

Then his friends shouted from the ice. “Elias! Come and race us!”

Just for a moment, he forgot his promise. He began to run.

His boot hit a patch of black ice. His feet flew up, and the globe flew out of his arms.

It struck the cobbles with a sound like a hundred tiny bells breaking at once.

Elias stared in horror. A whole winter of the old man’s work, gone in one irreversible second.

Tiny skaters, tiny lanterns and a hundred intricate wheels lay glittering in the snow.

The music stopped. Every face in the square turned towards him.

Elias did not wait. He scrambled up and ran, past the staring faces, straight into the tower.

He took the stairs two at a time until he stood before the silver hourglass.

“I can fix it,” he gasped. “One hour. Nobody will ever know.”

Pip landed beside him, quiet and solemn. “You can turn it,” he said. “Nobody can stop you.”

“But look outside first,” Pip said softly. “Look at what this hour holds.”

Elias went to the little window and looked down.

Master Tobin was kneeling in the snow, gathering the pieces into his handkerchief, and the children knelt with him.

Someone had started the old song again, and the whole town was singing it softly, like a lullaby.

“If you turn the glass, the globe will be whole,” said Pip. “But you will still be a boy who ran away.”

“Some things are irreversible, Elias. A broken promise cannot be turned back. It can only be mended.”

“And you cannot redeem a mistake by hiding it. You redeem it by facing it.”

Elias looked at the hourglass for a long time. His conscience was no longer whispering. It was clear and calm.

Slowly, he took his hands away from the silver, and he walked back down the stairs.
