That evening, Elias climbed the tower one last time and laid the brass key in front of Pip.

“Keep it for the next child who needs it,” he said. “I don’t need to go back anymore. I’d rather go forward.”

Pip blinked his great amber eyes, and for once he did not look solemn at all. He looked proud.

When Elias came down, Master Tobin was waiting at the shop door with something in his hand.

It was a heavy iron key with a tiny clock carved on its handle: the key to the shop.

“My very last key,” said the old man, “for my apprentice. The clocks have been far too quiet without you.”

So the shop opened again, and every afternoon the ticking filled the street once more.

Elias still thinks about the hourglass sometimes. But now, when a good moment comes, he does not try to catch it.

He simply lets it linger, warm and bright, like lantern light on fresh snow. Then he lets it go.

Because every hour is fleeting, and every hour is a gift. And the best ones are the ones we live only once.
