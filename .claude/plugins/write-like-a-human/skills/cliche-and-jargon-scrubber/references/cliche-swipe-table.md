# Cliche Swipe Table

The core deliverable of this skill. Every phrase below is a documented AI or corporate-writing tell, common enough that a sharp reader (or a detector) flags it on sight. Each one is mapped to 2 to 3 specific alternatives. The alternatives are starting points, not a mechanical find-and-replace: always adapt to the actual sentence and, where possible, to the buyer's own vocabulary from `voice_profile.vocabulary_notes`.

Read every "alternative" as a direction, not a drop-in phrase. The real fix usually involves naming the specific thing the vague phrase was standing in for.

## How to use this table

1. Find the flagged phrase or its category.
2. Read the "why it's a tell" column so you understand what to avoid reproducing in the fix, not just which words to avoid.
3. Pick an alternative direction, then rewrite the sentence around a concrete detail from the actual draft. A generic alternative used without adapting it to context is still filler wearing a new coat.

## Category 1: Transitions and connective tissue

| Cliche phrase | Why it's a tell | Alternative 1 | Alternative 2 | Alternative 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Furthermore | Formal connector nobody uses in speech; signals filler is coming | Cut it, start the next sentence directly | "And" (if the sentences genuinely add to each other) | Restate the new fact as its own short sentence |
| Moreover | Same as "furthermore," slightly more archaic | Cut it | "On top of that" (only if genuinely conversational) | Use a period and let the next sentence stand alone |
| In addition | Generic list-connector, adds no meaning | Cut it | Name what specifically is being added | "Also" (sparingly, and only if it reads natural) |
| It's worth noting that | Hedges a point that should just be stated | Cut the hedge, state the point directly | Cut entirely; if it's worth noting, just note it | Replace with the actual reason it matters |
| It's important to note that | Same issue, more common in longer-form AI drafts | Cut the hedge | State the fact plainly | If genuinely important, say why, not that it is |
| In conclusion | Textbook AI closer, especially in short pieces | Cut it, just deliver the closing point | Open with a callback to the opening line instead | End on the strongest concrete detail, no label needed |
| To summarize | Same as "in conclusion" | Cut it | Just summarize without announcing that you're doing it | Use a short punch sentence instead of a labeled summary |
| That being said | Filler pivot, rarely earns its place | Cut it | "Still," (only if a real contrast follows) | Restate the tension directly: "but here's the catch" |
| At the end of the day | Cliche pivot to a "real talk" moment | Cut it | State the bottom line directly | "What actually matters is..." |
| All things considered | Vague hedge before a conclusion | Cut it | State the conclusion without the preamble | Name the specific factors that were considered |

## Category 2: Hedges and empty qualifiers

| Cliche phrase | Why it's a tell | Alternative 1 | Alternative 2 | Alternative 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One could argue that | Distances the writer from their own claim | State the claim directly, own it | "I think" (if genuinely a personal opinion) | Cut the hedge, back the claim with a specific reason |
| It could be said that | Same distancing move | State it directly | Attribute it to a real source if there is one | Cut entirely |
| Arguably | Weak hedge, often unnecessary | Cut it | Replace with the actual evidence for the claim | State the claim, let the reasoning carry the weight |
| In many ways | Vague hedge that avoids specifics | Name the specific ways | Cut it, state the strongest way directly | Replace with a concrete example |
| To some extent | Non-committal qualifier | State the actual extent (a number, a scope) | Cut it if the sentence works without it | Replace with a specific limit or condition |
| Generally speaking | Signals the writer won't commit to specifics | Cut it | State the actual general pattern with a number or example | Name the exception instead of hedging the rule |
| It goes without saying | If it goes without saying, don't say it | Cut the whole phrase and the sentence after it, or just state the point | Cut entirely | Replace with the actual reason it's obvious |
| Needless to say | Same as above | Cut it | State the point without the announcement | Cut entirely |
| Some might say | Vague, unattributed hedge | Attribute to a real person or group | Cut it, state the perspective directly | Replace with a specific counterargument you'll address |

## Category 3: Corporate jargon and business-speak

| Cliche phrase | Why it's a tell | Alternative 1 | Alternative 2 | Alternative 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leverage (as a verb) | Overused business-speak for "use" | Use | Put to work | Name the specific action taken |
| Utilize | Inflated synonym for "use" | Use | Name the specific tool or method | Cut and just describe the action |
| Synergy | Vague buzzword with no concrete meaning | Name the actual combined effect | Describe specifically what worked better together | Cut entirely if no real mechanism is being described |
| Streamline | Vague claim of improvement | Name the specific step removed or shortened | Describe the before/after directly | "Cut the extra steps" (if that's literally what happened) |
| Optimize | Vague claim of improvement | Name the specific metric that improved and by how much | Describe what changed concretely | "Fix" or "improve," with a specific detail attached |
| Bandwidth (meaning capacity) | Corporate metaphor overused past meaning | Time | Capacity, stated specifically ("two hours a week") | "Room on the schedule" |
| Circle back | Meeting-room filler | "Follow up on [specific thing]" | "Talk again on [date/topic]" | Cut and just say what happens next |
| Move the needle | Vague claim of impact | Name the specific metric affected | State the actual number moved | Describe the concrete outcome |
| Low-hanging fruit | Cliche metaphor for easy wins | Name the specific easy win | "The fastest fix" | Describe what makes it easy, specifically |
| Deep dive | Cliche for "detailed look" | "A close look at [specific thing]" | Just describe the depth of analysis directly | Cut the label, deliver the detail |
| Touch base | Meeting-room filler for "talk" | "Talk," "check in on [specific thing]" | Name the actual purpose of the conversation | Cut and state the actual ask |
| Value-add | Vague corporate phrase for benefit | Name the specific benefit | Describe what changes for the reader concretely | Cut the label, state the benefit directly |
| Best-in-class | Unverifiable superlative | Name the specific comparison or metric | Cut the claim unless backed by a source | Describe the specific capability instead of ranking it |
| Core competency | Corporate abstraction | Name the actual skill or capability | Describe what the team or product actually does well | Cut the jargon, state the specific strength |
| Actionable insights | Vague, overused analytics phrase | Name the actual action the data suggests | Describe the specific finding and what to do about it | Cut "actionable," state the insight and the next step |
| Drill down | Cliche for "examine closely" | "Look closer at [specific thing]" | Just examine it, no label needed | Name the specific level of detail being added |
| Ecosystem (used loosely) | Overused metaphor for "the group of related things" | Name the specific tools, people, or parts | Describe the actual relationships involved | Cut and be literal about what's connected to what |
| Take it to the next level | Vague claim of improvement with no specifics | Name the specific next step | Describe the concrete outcome of the improvement | Cut the cliche, state what actually changes |

| Cliche phrase | Why it's a tell | Alternative 1 | Alternative 2 | Alternative 3 |

| Landscape (as metaphor for a field/industry) | Vague spatial metaphor overused in AI writing | Name the actual field or market directly | "The industry," "the market," used plainly | Describe the specific conditions instead of the metaphor |
| Multifaceted | Vague claim of complexity | Name the specific facets | Describe 2 to 3 concrete dimensions instead of the label | Cut the label, show the complexity through detail |
| Comprehensive | Overused claim of completeness, often inaccurate | State the actual scope ("covers 12 topics," "40 pages") | "Complete" only if verifiably true | Cut and let the actual content prove the scope |
| Robust | Vague strength claim | Name the specific capability that makes it strong | Describe what it withstands or handles, specifically | "Solid," only with a concrete reason attached |
| Unlock (as metaphor for enable) | Overused AI metaphor | "Enable," "make possible" | Name the specific outcome directly | Describe what becomes possible, concretely |
| Unleash | Same issue, more intense version | Name the specific outcome | Cut the metaphor entirely | Describe the concrete result plainly |
| Empower | Vague claim about giving ability | Name the specific capability granted | Describe what the person can now do, concretely | "Give [person] the ability to [specific action]" |
| Bespoke | Overused faux-luxury word for "custom" | "Custom," "built for [specific situation]" | Describe the specific customization | Name what's actually tailored and how |
| Journey (as metaphor for a process) | Overused, especially in marketing and self-help contexts | Name the actual process or timeline | Describe the specific stages concretely | Cut the metaphor, state what happens and when |
| Navigate (as metaphor for handle/manage) | Overused spatial metaphor | "Handle," "manage," "work through" | Describe the specific challenge and response | Cut the metaphor, state the action plainly |
| Seamless | Vague, often unverifiable smoothness claim | Describe specifically what connects and how | Name what friction was removed | Cut and let the actual mechanism speak for itself |
| Revolutionize | Wildly overused, rarely earned | Name the specific, concrete change | "Change," scoped to what actually changed | Cut the hype, state the real impact with a number |

| Groundbreaking | Overused, rarely verified | Name what specifically is new or first | Cut unless genuinely first-of-its-kind and provable | Describe the actual innovation concretely |
| Embark (on a journey/on a mission) | Overused flourish for "start" | "Start," "begin" | Name the specific first step | Cut the flourish, state the action plainly |
| Beacon (of hope/innovation) | Overused metaphor, especially in mission statements | Name what the thing actually does | Cut the metaphor entirely | Describe the concrete role or function |
| Paradigm shift | Overused, rarely an accurate description of the actual change | Name the specific change in approach | "Change," scoped to what actually shifted | Describe the before and after concretely |
| Holistic | Vague claim of completeness | Name the specific parts being addressed together | Describe the actual scope, concretely | Cut the label, list what's included |
| Innovative | Overused, unverifiable on its own | Name the specific new element | Describe what's different from the standard approach | Cut and let the specific detail carry the claim |
| Game changer | Cliche claim of impact | Name the specific change in outcome or behavior | Describe what's different because of this, concretely | Cut the cliche, state the real effect with a number |
| Cutting-edge | Vague, quickly dated claim | Name the specific technology or method | Describe what makes it new, concretely | Cut and let the specifics prove the claim |
| World-class | Unverifiable superlative | Name the specific comparison or credential | Cut unless backed by an actual ranking or result | Describe the specific capability instead |

## Category 5: Full-phrase AI cliches

| Cliche phrase | Why it's a tell | Alternative 1 | Alternative 2 | Alternative 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| In today's fast-paced world | The single most flagged AI opener | Cut it entirely, open with the actual point | Name the specific pressure or condition directly | Open with a concrete scenario instead of a generic claim |
| In today's digital age | Same issue, digital-specific version | Cut it entirely | Name the specific technology or shift directly | Open with a concrete example of the change |
| Navigate the landscape (of X) | Combines two flagged metaphors into one | "Work in [specific field]" | Name the specific challenges directly | Cut the metaphor, describe the actual terrain concretely |
| Delve into the world of | Combines "delve" with a vague scope claim | "Look at," "get into," naming the actual topic | Cut it, start with the actual first point | State the specific angle being covered |
| Let's dive in | Overused casual opener that adds nothing | Cut it, start with the first real point | Open with a question or a concrete claim instead | Cut entirely and let the content start |
| A tapestry of (X) | Overused metaphor, usually describing a mix | Name the specific elements in the mix | "A mix of [specific things]" | Cut the metaphor, list the specifics |
| It's not just X, it's Y | Extremely overused rhetorical structure | State the actual point directly without the frame | Name the specific distinction concretely | Use only if the contrast is genuinely surprising, and vary the structure |
| Designed to enhance | Vague, passive marketing phrase | Name the specific improvement and mechanism | "Built to [specific outcome]" | Describe what actually changes for the user |
| Whether you're a beginner or an expert | Overused hedge trying to address every reader at once | Pick the actual primary reader and write to them | Name the two specific situations separately with different advice | Cut and address the real, narrower audience directly |
| The possibilities are endless | Vague, unverifiable closer | Name 2 to 3 specific possibilities | Cut the cliche, end on the strongest concrete example | Replace with a specific, real next step |
| At its core | Filler lead-in to a point that could just be stated | Cut it, state the point directly | Name the specific core mechanism | Cut and start with the concrete detail |
| When it comes to (X) | Overused throat-clearing lead-in | Cut it, name the topic directly as the subject | Restructure the sentence to lead with the actual subject | Cut entirely |
| The bottom line is | Cliche closer, especially in business writing | State the conclusion directly, no label | Cut it, let the conclusion stand alone | Replace with the specific number or outcome that matters |
| Now more than ever | Vague urgency claim with no real basis | Name the specific reason urgency exists now | Cut the cliche, state the actual timely reason | Replace with a concrete, current detail |
| Only time will tell | Filler closer that says nothing | Cut it, end on the actual open question | State what specifically remains uncertain | Cut and end on the strongest known fact instead |

## Notes on use

- Words like "comprehensive," "robust," and "innovative" are not banned outright, they are earned only by a specific, checkable detail right next to them. Flag them, then check whether the sentence backs the claim with a number, a name, or a mechanism. If it does, the word may survive. If it doesn't, cut or replace it.
- Full-phrase cliches (Category 5) should be cut entirely far more often than replaced, since most of them add zero information and exist purely as connective filler or hedge.
- Always check the buyer's `voice_profile.vocabulary_notes` before defaulting to the alternatives above; their real words beat any generic suggestion in this table.
