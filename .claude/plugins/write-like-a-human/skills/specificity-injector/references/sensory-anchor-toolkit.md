# Sensory Anchor Toolkit

Five categories of concrete detail. Every rewritten sentence in this skill's output should land in at least one of these. This file shows what each category looks like and how to fit it into a sentence without it reading like a fact dropped in from a spreadsheet.

## 1. Numbers

The most common anchor and the easiest to overuse. A number works when it is specific enough to be checkable and rare enough to stand out.

- Weak number: "increased by a lot" (not a number at all).
- Working number: "went from 12 clients to 31 in eight months."
- Strong number: "went from 12 clients to 31, and I did it with the same three-person team."

**Placement rule:** put the number where the sentence's stress naturally falls, usually right after the verb, not buried in a subordinate clause. "Revenue grew 40% in Q1" lands harder than "In Q1, among other changes, revenue experienced 40% growth."

**Overuse warning:** a paragraph with five numbers in five sentences reads like a spec sheet, not a person. Pick the one or two numbers that carry the paragraph's actual point and let the rest of the sentences carry texture instead.

## 2. Named examples (real or specific-but-anonymized)

A name, real or a specific descriptor, turns "a business" into a business the reader can picture.

- Weak: "a client of mine"
- Working (anonymized but specific): "a 40-person agency I worked with last spring"
- Working (real, with permission): "[CLIENT NAME], who runs a wedding photography studio outside [CITY]"

**Anonymizing without going vague:** the goal isn't to hide the specific, it's to hide the identity while keeping the texture. "A 40-person agency" still tells the reader size and type. "A business" tells them nothing. Always keep at least one identifying trait (size, industry, location, timeframe) even when the name itself is withheld.

**Placement rule:** introduce the named example early in the sentence or paragraph, then refer back to it briefly ("that same client," "she," "the team") rather than re-explaining who they are every time.

## 3. Sensory anchors

Physical, sensory, or situational detail: what something looked like, sounded like, felt like, or where it happened. This is the category AI drafts skip almost entirely, because a model has no lived sensory memory to draw on, which makes it one of the most effective categories for sounding human.

- Weak: "the launch didn't go smoothly"
- Working: "the checkout page crashed four minutes after we hit send, and I watched the error logs fill up faster than I could read them"
- Weak: "the office was busy"
- Working: "every desk had two monitors and someone's cold coffee next to the keyboard"

**Where to use it:** sensory anchors are most powerful in openers, story moments, and the emotional peak of an anecdote. They are usually wrong for a summary sentence or a closer; save them for the scene, not the takeaway.

**Sourcing rule:** never invent a sensory detail wholesale. If the buyer hasn't given you one, ask the Type 4 (sensory or scene) question from `specificity-interview-framework.md`, or soften the sentence instead of inventing texture that didn't happen.

## 4. Timeframes

A specific window of time does two things a vague one can't: it makes the claim checkable, and it implies the writer actually remembers the event rather than summarizing a trend.

- Weak: "recently" / "over time" / "for a while"
- Working: "the second week of January" / "eight months in" / "the Tuesday before the product launched"

**Precision level:** you don't need calendar-exact precision, you need enough specificity that the sentence sounds remembered rather than generalized. "Last spring" beats "recently." "The week our biggest client almost left" beats "a difficult period."

**Placement rule:** timeframes work best near the front of a sentence, setting the scene before the claim: "Eight months into working with her, the pricing finally clicked" reads more human than "The pricing finally clicked after eight months of working with her, which shows how..." (the second trails into hedge territory).

## 5. Named tools, places, and processes

Specific nouns, the actual tool, platform, city, or process name, do quiet work that generic category words can't. "A project management tool" could be anything. "A shared spreadsheet that nobody updated" is a specific, slightly embarrassing truth.

- Weak: "using the right software"
- Working: "moving off a shared spreadsheet and into [SPECIFIC TOOL, per buyer]"
- Weak: "in a major city"
- Working: "in [SPECIFIC CITY OR NEIGHBORHOOD, per buyer]"
- Weak: "following a proven process"
- Working: "running the same three-question intake call every single time"

**Sourcing rule:** tool and place names must come from the buyer or from the draft itself. Never substitute a well-known brand name as a generic stand-in ("like Google Docs") unless the buyer actually used it; if unsure, ask or use a generic-but-specific description instead ("a shared doc," "a Tuesday call").

## Combining anchors without overloading a sentence

One anchor per sentence is usually enough. Two is fine if they're doing different jobs (a number plus a timeframe: "31 clients in eight months"). Three or more in one sentence starts to read like a résumé bullet. Spread anchors across a paragraph rather than stacking them in one line:

**Overloaded:** "In the second week of January, a 40-person agency I worked with in Denver used our three-step process to grow from 12 to 31 clients in eight months using a shared spreadsheet we built together."

**Better, spread across two sentences:** "A 40-person agency I worked with hit a wall in January: they were still tracking leads in a shared spreadsheet nobody trusted. Eight months later, using the same three-step process, they'd gone from 12 clients to 31."
