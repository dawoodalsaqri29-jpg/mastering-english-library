# Vague to Specific Gallery

Twenty-four worked before/after pairs, organized by the type of fix. Use this to calibrate what "vague" actually means and to see the range of moves available before you default to just adding a number.

Every "after" example below uses a bracketed placeholder where a real specific from the buyer would go. When you use this gallery on a real draft, replace the bracket with the buyer's actual answer, never with an invented value.

## Category 1: Abstract noun phrases replaced with a concrete verb or noun

Abstract noun phrases ("the implementation of," "the utilization of," "the optimization of") are one of the clearest predictability tells in AI-generated writing. They let a sentence sound important while saying almost nothing. The fix is usually to find the verb hiding inside the noun and use it.

1. **Before:** "The implementation of the new onboarding process led to improvements in retention."
   **After:** "We rebuilt onboarding in [MONTH]. Retention went up [X]% in the first [TIMEFRAME]."

2. **Before:** "Through the utilization of automation, the team was able to reduce manual work."
   **After:** "We automated the [SPECIFIC TASK]. That freed up about [X hours per week] the team used to lose to it."

3. **Before:** "The optimization of our content strategy resulted in increased engagement."
   **After:** "We cut our posting schedule from daily to [X] times a week and doubled down on [FORMAT]. Engagement went up [X]%."

4. **Before:** "Facilitation of cross-team communication was a key priority."
   **After:** "We started a [X]-minute standup every [DAY]. It's the only meeting nobody complains about."

5. **Before:** "The organization prioritized the enhancement of customer experience."
   **After:** "[COMPANY NAME OR DESCRIPTOR] rewrote its [SPECIFIC TOUCHPOINT, e.g. "return policy email"] so it took [X] steps instead of [Y]."

## Category 2: Unquantified claims replaced with a real number

"Many," "significant," "substantial," and "a lot of" are doing the work a number should be doing. If the buyer has any figure, even an approximate one, it beats the vague word.

6. **Before:** "Many small businesses struggle with cash flow."
   **After:** "[X]% of the small businesses I've worked with run out of runway before they run out of ideas." (Or, without a stat: "Three of my last five clients called me in a panic about payroll, not about sales.")

7. **Before:** "This approach led to a significant increase in conversions."
   **After:** "Conversions went from [X]% to [Y]% over [TIMEFRAME]."

8. **Before:** "A substantial portion of our audience prefers video content."
   **After:** "[X] out of every [Y] people who open our newsletter click through to the video first, before the text."

9. **Before:** "We've helped a lot of clients achieve their goals."
   **After:** "We've worked with [X] clients since [YEAR]. [Y] of them are still on retainer."

10. **Before:** "Response times improved considerably."
    **After:** "Average response time dropped from [X hours] to [Y minutes]."

## Category 3: Unnamed examples replaced with a specific (or specific-but-anonymized) one

You don't need a real name to be specific. A believable, specific noun phrase ("a 12-person marketing team," "a solo bakery in [CITY]") does almost as much work as a real name, and it's the right call when the buyer wants to protect a client's privacy.

11. **Before:** "A client of mine was dealing with a difficult situation."
    **After:** "[CLIENT DESCRIPTOR, e.g. "a 40-person agency"] I worked with last [SEASON] had lost their biggest account and needed a new positioning story in under two weeks."

12. **Before:** "One business saw great results from this strategy."
    **After:** "[BUSINESS DESCRIPTOR, e.g. "a family-run hardware store"] went from [X] to [Y] using this exact approach."

13. **Before:** "Some companies in this space have found success with a niche focus."
    **After:** "[COMPANY DESCRIPTOR] stopped trying to serve everyone and picked one industry. Revenue [SPECIFIC OUTCOME] within [TIMEFRAME]."

14. **Before:** "Experts in the field recommend this technique."
    **After:** "[NAMED PERSON OR SPECIFIC ROLE, e.g. "the head of growth at a Series B startup I consulted for"] told me this was the first thing she'd fix."

15. **Before:** "Customers have reported positive feedback."
    **After:** "The line I hear most often, almost word for word, is: '[SPECIFIC QUOTE OR PARAPHRASE FROM BUYER].'"

## Category 4: Vague timeframes replaced with a tight window

"Recently," "for a while," and "over time" let a claim float free of any timeline a reader could check or picture. A tight window makes the claim feel lived, not asserted.

16. **Before:** "We recently made some changes to our process."
    **After:** "We changed this on [MONTH/DATE OR "the first week of Q2"]."

17. **Before:** "This has been a challenge for a while now."
    **After:** "This has been the same fight for about [X months/years], since [SPECIFIC TRIGGER EVENT, e.g. "the platform changed its algorithm"]."

18. **Before:** "Over time, we noticed a pattern."
    **After:** "By the third [WEEK/MONTH/PROJECT], the same problem showed up every single time."

19. **Before:** "It took some time to figure out."
    **After:** "It took [X weeks/attempts] before we found the actual cause."

## Category 5: Hedge-heavy generalizations replaced with a specific instance or a plainly stated opinion

AI drafts default to hedged, flat, resolved statements ("can often," "may sometimes," "tends to") because hedging is the statistically safest thing to say. A specific instance, or a flatly stated opinion the buyer actually holds, reads as human because it takes a position.

20. **Before:** "This strategy can often lead to better outcomes for businesses of various sizes."
    **After:** "This worked for a [X-PERSON] team and it worked for a solo [ROLE]. The size didn't matter. The follow-through did."

21. **Before:** "Results may vary depending on a range of factors."
    **After:** "[SPECIFIC FACTOR, e.g. "if you don't have someone answering DMs within an hour"] is the thing that actually decides whether this works."

22. **Before:** "It's important to consider several variables before making a decision."
    **After:** "Before you touch pricing, check [SPECIFIC THING #1] and [SPECIFIC THING #2]. Everything else is noise."

23. **Before:** "There are many benefits to this approach worth exploring."
    **After:** "The one benefit that actually matters: [SPECIFIC BENEFIT, stated plainly, no hedge]."

24. **Before:** "This tends to work well in most cases."
    **After:** "This has worked every time I've tried it except once, when [SPECIFIC EXCEPTION]."

## When to leave a generality alone

Not every general statement needs a fix. Leave it if:
- It is a genuine, deliberate summary sentence that follows three or four specific ones (a "so here's the takeaway" line earns the right to generalize once).
- Making it specific would require the buyer to reveal something they've explicitly said they want to keep vague (a client's identity, an exact revenue number).
- It is a transition or signpost sentence with no claim in it ("Here's what changed next.").

The test: does this sentence make a claim a skeptical reader could ask "based on what?" If yes and it's currently unanswerable, fix it. If no, leave it.
