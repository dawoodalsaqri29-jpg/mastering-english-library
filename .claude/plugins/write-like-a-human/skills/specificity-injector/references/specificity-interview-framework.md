# Specificity Interview Framework

The rule this whole skill exists to enforce: never invent a fake number, name, date, or story to make a sentence sound concrete. When a draft needs a specific the buyer hasn't given you, ask for it. This file is the exact, minimal-friction way to ask.

## Why minimal-friction matters

A buyer who wanted to answer twenty questions would have written the specific draft themselves. The interview has to feel like a fast, useful exchange, not a form. Two rules make that true:

1. **Anchor every question to the exact sentence it will fix.** Never ask an abstract question like "tell me about your results." Ask "you wrote 'this led to significant improvements,' what's the real number, even roughly?"
2. **Batch 3 to 5 questions at a time**, grouped by the section of the draft they came from, not fired one at a time in a slow back-and-forth.

## The five question types

Every vague claim needs one of five kinds of specific. Match the question type to the claim.

### Type 1: The number question
Use when the vague word is a quantifier ("many," "significant," "most," "a lot").

**Template:** "You wrote '[QUOTE THE VAGUE SENTENCE].' Do you have a real number here, even approximate? If not, is there a comparison that gets the point across (like '3 out of my last 5 clients')?"

**Example:** "You wrote 'this saved a significant amount of time.' Do you know roughly how many hours a week, or is there a before/after you remember (like 'used to take all Friday, now takes an hour')?"

### Type 2: The named-example question
Use when the claim references an unnamed client, business, or person.

**Template:** "You mentioned '[QUOTE THE VAGUE REFERENCE].' Can I use a real name, or would you rather I use a specific but anonymous descriptor (like 'a 12-person marketing team' or 'a bakery in the Midwest')? Either way, what's one detail that makes this example memorable, like their size, location, or what made the situation unusual?"

**Example:** "You mentioned 'a client I worked with.' Real name okay, or should I keep them anonymous? And what's one detail, like their industry or team size, that would help this land?"

### Type 3: The timeframe question
Use when the claim floats on "recently," "over time," or "for a while."

**Template:** "You wrote '[QUOTE THE VAGUE SENTENCE].' Roughly when was this, or what triggered it? Even a season or a rough month is more specific than 'recently.'"

**Example:** "You wrote 'we recently changed our approach.' Was that this quarter, or further back? What actually kicked it off?"

### Type 4: The sensory or scene question
Use when a claim describes an event or moment with no texture (what did it look like, sound like, feel like).

**Template:** "You mentioned '[QUOTE THE MOMENT].' What's one specific detail you remember from that, something you saw, heard, or felt, that would make a reader feel like they were there?"

**Example:** "You mentioned 'the launch didn't go as planned.' What actually happened, specifically, in that first hour?"

### Type 5: The opinion question
Use when a claim is hedged into meaninglessness ("can often," "may sometimes," "in many cases").

**Template:** "You wrote '[QUOTE THE HEDGED CLAIM].' What do you actually think here, no hedge? If you had to bet, what's the real answer?"

**Example:** "You wrote 'results can vary depending on several factors.' If someone asked you point-blank what actually determines whether this works, what would you say?"

## Batching the interview

Group flagged claims by where they sit in the draft (opening, middle, closer) and present 3 to 5 at once:

> "A few spots in this draft could use a real detail instead of a general claim. Quick round, answer what you can, skip anything you don't have handy:
> 1. [Type 1 question]
> 2. [Type 2 question]
> 3. [Type 3 question]
> 4. [Type 4 question]"

Never send more than 5 in one batch. If the draft has more than 8 to 10 flagged claims, do two batches: fix the highest-impact ones first (the opening line, the core proof point, the closer), then circle back for the rest only if the buyer has appetite.

## The skip-and-soften protocol

If the buyer skips a question or says "I don't have that," do not:
- Leave the original vague sentence as-is and move on silently.
- Make up a plausible-sounding number or name.

Instead, do one of these, and say out loud which one you did:
- **Soften to honest generality.** Strip the false authority. "Studies show this works" (unverifiable, sounds authoritative) becomes "in my experience, this works" (honest, still true, no longer pretending to cite data it doesn't have).
- **Cut the sentence.** If the claim isn't earning its place without a specific, the draft is stronger without it. Say so: "Cut this line since we didn't have a real detail to back it and it wasn't adding anything the surrounding sentences didn't already cover."
- **Generalize on purpose, once.** If this is a summary line following three specific ones already in the paragraph, it's allowed to stay general. Note that you left it as a deliberate wrap-up line, not an oversight.

## Full worked mini-interview

Draft excerpt: "Many freelancers struggle with pricing their work. I've seen this play out with clients over the years, and the results speak for themselves once they make the shift."

Flagged: "many freelancers" (Type 1), "clients... over the years" (Type 2 and Type 3), "the results speak for themselves" (Type 1, vague outcome).

Batch sent to buyer:
> "Three quick ones on that pricing paragraph:
> 1. You wrote 'many freelancers struggle with pricing.' Any rough sense of how many, or a specific pattern you see (like '9 out of 10 people who come to me are under-charging by half')?
> 2. You mentioned 'clients over the years.' Got one specific client or type of client in mind, even anonymized, that's the clearest example?
> 3. 'The results speak for themselves,' what actually changed for them, roughly?"

Buyer answers: "Honestly like 90% of people I talk to are underpricing. One client, a wedding photographer, went from charging $800 a shoot to $2,200 within six months once we fixed her positioning."

Rewrite: "About 90% of the freelancers I talk to are underpricing, badly. One client, a wedding photographer, was charging $800 a shoot. Six months after we fixed her positioning, she was at $2,200, same client list, same skill, just priced like she meant it."

Note the shift: one real answer supplied both the number and the example, and the rewrite used the buyer's own phrase ("priced like she meant it" implied by "once we fixed her positioning") to keep it in their voice rather than a generic report-out.
