# Story Weaver: Worked Example

Sample brand (for illustration only): Dax Correro, who owns a small craft brewery and writes a monthly blog post for the taproom's newsletter. He drafted this post with an AI tool to explain a recipe change and it reads flat and generic, with no sense that an actual person runs this place.

## Input draft (pasted by the buyer)

"Choosing the right recipe for a new beer release is an important part of running a successful brewery. Many breweries face the challenge of balancing customer preferences with creative vision. It's important to test new recipes and gather feedback before committing to a full batch. This approach can lead to better outcomes and increased customer satisfaction. We recently made some changes to our process and we're excited about the results."

## Step 1: Diagnose the gaps

Flagged: a flat, generic opener with no scene; a central claim ("test before you commit to a full batch") asserted with zero story behind it; and an abrupt, unearned closer ("we recently made some changes... excited about the results") that reads like a press release, not an owner talking to regulars.

## Step 2: Check what the buyer has

Dax says: "Yeah, actually, we had a bad batch story. Our hazy IPA. It didn't sell."

Thin answer, needs mining.

## Step 3: Anecdote-mining interview (Failure story type, batched)

Sent to Dax:
> "A few quick ones on that hazy IPA batch:
> 1. What's the moment you realized it was going wrong, specifically?
> 2. What did it cost you, concretely, time or money or something else?
> 3. What did you do right after you realized it, the very next thing?
> 4. What's one specific detail from that day you still remember?"

Dax's answers: "I was behind the bar and three people in a row ordered something else instead of the hazy. It cost us about eleven grand in ingredients and tank time for that batch. Right after, I called my business partner and just said 'we need to test before we brew a full batch ever again.' The detail I remember is her face, she didn't say 'I told you so' but she also didn't need to."

## Step 4: Shape into situation, tension, action, result

Situation: standing behind the bar, watching the new release. Tension: three people in a row skip it, the batch cost eleven grand, his partner's reaction. Action: called his partner, committed to testing before full batches. Result: became the standard process, explained later in the piece.

## Step 5: Story-shaped opener (Cold open pattern)

"Three people in a row ordered something else."

## Steps 6 to 7: Weave and build transitions

**Final rewritten draft delivered to Dax:**

"Three people in a row ordered something else. I was behind the bar watching our new hazy IPA get passed over, one pour at a time, doing the math in my head on how much that batch had actually cost us. Eleven grand, it turned out, in ingredients and tank time for a beer that was going to sit on tap until it went flat.

I called my business partner that night. I didn't have a plan yet, just one sentence: we can't brew a full batch again without testing it first. She didn't say 'I told you so.' She didn't have to. Her face said it for her, and honestly, that was worse.

So now every new recipe gets a twenty-person test pour before it touches a tank. It's slower. It means some releases get pushed back a few weeks while we figure out if a beer is actually good or just good in my head. But we haven't eaten an eleven-thousand-dollar batch since, and more than that, the beers that do make it to a full brew sell out instead of sitting there.

That's the whole system. Not a fancy process, just the rule we made the night three people in a row walked past our own beer."

**Note to buyer:** The eleven-thousand-dollar figure, the twenty-person test pour detail, and the sequence of events all come from Dax's own account. Flagged per his fact-guard setting (moderate) for him to confirm the dollar amount before this goes out, since it's the one hard number in the piece.
