# Anecdote Mining Interview

Fourteen questions organized by story type. The rule behind every question here: dig past what happened into what it felt like, what specific detail is still vivid, who else was there, and what almost went wrong. Bare facts make a summary. Sensory and emotional detail makes a story.

Ask in one batch of 3 to 5 questions from a single story type, matched to the gap in the draft. Never fire all fourteen at once. If an answer comes back thin ("it just worked out" or "it was hard, then it got better"), ask exactly one follow-up for texture before moving on: "What's one specific moment from that you still remember?" usually gets it out of them.

## Story type 1: Origin story

Use for openers, "why I do this work" sections, or establishing credibility without a résumé dump.

1. What were you doing, or what was going on in your life, right before this thing started? Set the scene: where were you, what did an ordinary day look like then?
2. What's the specific moment you can point to as the trigger, a conversation, an event, a realization? Not the general reason, the actual moment.
3. What did you think was going to happen, versus what you were actually afraid might happen?
4. Who else was in the room or in the story with you at that moment, and what did they say or do?
5. Looking back, what's one detail (something you saw, something someone said, something you were wearing or holding) that you still remember clearly?

**Example answer (career coach, Q2 and Q5):** "I was sitting in a Zoom call being laid off, and my manager said 'this isn't about your performance' in the exact same flat tone she used to announce the coffee machine was broken. I remember thinking that was the moment I decided I'd never let someone else control the timeline of my career again."

## Story type 2: Failure story

Use for turning points, "here's what I learned" sections, or building trust through honesty rather than polish.

6. What's something you tried that didn't work, that you were pretty confident about going in?
7. What was the moment you realized it was going wrong, specifically, not "eventually" but the actual instant you knew?
8. What did it cost you, concretely: time, money, a relationship, your own confidence?
9. What did you do right after that moment, the very next action, not the eventual recovery?

**Example answer (craft brewery owner, Q7 and Q8):** "We'd bet the whole spring release on a hazy IPA and I remember standing in the tasting room watching three people in a row order something else. That batch cost us about eleven grand in ingredients and taproom time, and worse, it was the first time my business partner looked at me like maybe I didn't actually know what I was doing."

## Story type 3: Turning point

Use for the pivot in a longer piece, the "and then everything changed" beat, or a before/after structure.

10. What was true before, that stopped being true after? State the specific before and after, not just "things got better."
11. What's the exact decision or action that marked the turn, something you could point to on a calendar?
12. Was there a moment of doubt right before you committed to the change? What almost stopped you?

**Example answer (solo attorney, Q11 and Q12):** "I turned down a referral from my old firm, my biggest source of work at the time, to take on my first flat-fee client instead. I almost didn't. I remember sitting in my car in the parking lot for probably ten minutes before I called the referral back to say no."

## Story type 4: Client or customer story

Use for proof points and case-study-shaped sections, without relying on a testimonial quote alone.

13. Think of one specific client or customer whose situation stuck with you. What was going on for them when they first came to you? (Get a role, industry, or descriptor, not necessarily a name.)
14. What almost went wrong, or what was the hardest part of working with them? What was the moment, or the specific result, that told you it had worked?

**Example answer (career coach, Q13 and Q14):** "An accountant, mid-thirties, had been quietly job hunting for two years without telling anyone at her firm. The hardest part was she kept sabotaging her own interviews by undervaluing her experience. The moment I knew it had clicked was when she sent me a text after a final-round interview that just said 'I didn't apologize for my salary number once.'"

## Story type 5: Small daily-life moment

Use for lighter transitions, humanizing an otherwise dense section, or a shorter piece that doesn't have room for a full arc.

Use any 2 to 3 of these, they're smaller asks than the other categories:

- What's a small, ordinary moment from this week or last that actually relates to this topic, even loosely? (A commute, a conversation, something a kid or a coworker said.)
- Is there a repeated, almost mundane habit you have that connects to this point (a specific routine, a recurring Tuesday call, a running joke with a colleague)?
- What's something someone said to you recently, in passing, that you're still thinking about?

**Example answer (career coach):** "My neighbor asked me last week what I actually do for work, and I found myself saying 'I basically get paid to tell smart people they're allowed to want more,' which is truer than anything on my website."

## Handling a thin or reluctant answer

If the buyer gives a one-line, low-detail answer, ask exactly one of these follow-ups, matched to what's missing:

- Missing sensory detail: "What's one specific thing you saw, heard, or remember physically from that moment?"
- Missing stakes: "What would have happened if it hadn't worked out?"
- Missing a real ending: "How did it actually resolve, not the lesson, just what literally happened next?"

If the second answer is still thin, stop pushing. Use what you have, keep the story short, and do not pad it with invented detail to make it feel fuller than it is. A three-sentence real story beats a ten-sentence embellished one.
