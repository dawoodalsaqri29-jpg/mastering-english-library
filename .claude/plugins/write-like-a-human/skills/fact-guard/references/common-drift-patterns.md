# Common Drift Patterns

Eight named ways a humanization pass accidentally drifts from the facts in the original. Recognizing the pattern by name makes it faster to spot in a live comparison, and faster to explain to a buyer why something got flagged. Each pattern includes a before and after example and the fix.

## 1. The Confident Rounder

A precise number gets rounded toward a cleaner, more rhetorically satisfying figure, and the rounding direction always seems to favor the more impressive version.

**Before:** "We reduced churn by 18%."
**After:** "We cut churn by nearly a fifth."
**Why it happens:** "Nearly a fifth" sounds more natural spoken aloud than "18%," so a rewrite reaching for conversational rhythm reaches for the round number. But 18% is not "nearly a fifth" (20%) and the gap is large enough to matter in a report a reader might rely on.
**Fix:** Keep the precise figure. If a rounded, spoken-language version is wanted for flow, pair it correctly: "18%, call it one in five."

## 2. The Invented Specific

A vague-but-true claim gets a fabricated concrete detail bolted onto it because specific details read as more human and more trustworthy, which is exactly the instinct that makes this pattern dangerous.

**Before:** "A client saw results within a few weeks."
**After:** "A client in the Pacific Northwest saw a 27% lift in nineteen days."

**Why it happens:** Rewrites asked to "add specificity" or "sound less generic" will generate a plausible-sounding number, place, or name to fill the gap, because a specific-sounding sentence pattern-matches to good writing even when the specifics are fabricated.
**Fix:** Never let a rewrite add a number, place, name, or timeframe that was not in the original. If the piece genuinely needs more specificity, that has to come from the buyer supplying the real detail, not from the rewrite inventing one.

## 3. The Softened Claim

A qualifier gets added that was not in the original, weakening a claim the buyer actually stands behind, usually in the name of sounding less "salesy" or more humble.

**Before:** "This process eliminates duplicate data entry."
**After:** "This process can help reduce duplicate data entry in many cases."

**Why it happens:** Humanizing passes sometimes over-correct toward hedging because hedged language can sound more natural and less like marketing copy. But if the buyer's original claim was accurate and intended as a flat statement, softening it changes the claim without the buyer's consent.
**Fix:** Only add a hedge if the original claim was already hedged or if the buyer confirms the stronger version was an overstatement. Do not hedge by default in the name of tone.

## 4. The Strengthened Claim

The opposite of the softened claim: a modest, hedged original gets rewritten into something more absolute, usually because absolute language reads punchier.

**Before:** "This approach has worked well for many of our clients."
**After:** "This approach works."

**Why it happens:** Cutting hedges is a legitimate humanizing move (real humans do not say "in many cases" constantly), but cutting the hedge and the scope together turns a qualified claim into a universal one, which is a factual overstatement, not a style fix.
**Fix:** When trimming a hedge for rhythm, check whether the hedge was carrying factual weight (scope, frequency, certainty) or just verbal filler. Only cut the ones that are pure filler.

## 5. The Dropped Caveat

A caveat that scoped or limited a claim disappears while the claim itself survives, changing what the remaining claim actually means.

**Before:** "Setup takes about 15 minutes, assuming your team already has admin access."
**After:** "Setup takes about 15 minutes."

**Why it happens:** Caveats are often the first thing cut when a rewrite is told to tighten a sentence, because they read as clutter. But a caveat like "assuming your team already has admin access" is load-bearing: without it, the 15-minute claim is simply less true for a large share of readers.
**Fix:** Before cutting any clause that starts with "assuming," "excluding," "unless," "based on," or "in cases where," check whether it is narrowing the claim's truth value. If it is, keep it or fold it into the sentence differently, but do not delete it.

## 6. The Quote Paraphrase

A direct quote gets rewritten into a paraphrase but is still presented with quotation marks or as an attributed statement, misrepresenting what the person actually said.

**Before:** A customer said, "it paid for itself in the first month."
**After:** A customer said it was worth every penny right away.

**Why it happens:** Rewrites smooth quotes to fit the surrounding voice, but a quote is not supposed to match the surrounding voice; it is supposed to be what the person actually said.
**Fix:** Direct quotes are never rewritten for style. If a quote reads stiffly and the buyer wants it to flow better, either keep it verbatim and adjust the sentence around it, or convert it explicitly to reported speech without quotation marks ("a customer said the tool paid for itself within a month").

## 7. The Silent Comparative Shift

A comparative or ranking claim shifts strength during a rewrite, usually toward something bolder, without the underlying evidence changing at all.

**Before:** "One of the fastest onboarding flows we've built."
**After:** "The fastest onboarding flow we've built."

**Why it happens:** "One of the fastest" is a hedge-shaped phrase that reads clunkier than a clean superlative, so a rewrite chasing punchier prose quietly drops the "one of."
**Fix:** Treat "one of the [superlative]" as a claim type on its own. Never collapse it to a bare superlative during a style pass; that is a factual claim change disguised as a grammar fix.

## 8. The Merged Timeline

Two separate events or timeframes from the original get compressed into one during a rewrite that is trying to tighten pacing, creating a timeline that did not happen.

**Before:** "We piloted this with five clients in January, then rolled it out company-wide in April."
**After:** "We rolled this out in January."

**Why it happens:** Combining two sentences into one is a normal humanizing move for rhythm, but when the two sentences describe two different events at two different times, merging them erases one event and misdates the other.
**Fix:** When combining sentences that each contain a date or timeframe, check whether they describe the same event or two different ones. Only merge dates when they genuinely refer to the same milestone.

## Using this list during a review

Run through these eight names as a checklist any time a claim gets flagged as Altered, Dropped, or Invented. Naming the pattern (for example, "this is a Dropped Caveat, not just a shortened sentence") makes the flag concrete and gives the buyer language to recognize the same failure mode next time they review a rewrite themselves.
