# Paragraph Shape Patterns

Sentence-level rhythm is half the fix. The other half is paragraph shape. An AI draft that varies sentence length sentence by sentence can still read flat if every paragraph is the same length and covers ground the same way. This file is a bank of paragraph shapes to pull from so a longer piece does not read as a stack of identical blocks.

## The default AI shape (what you are moving away from)

Almost every AI-generated draft defaults to the same paragraph: three sentences, a topic sentence, a supporting sentence, a closing or transition sentence, roughly 50 to 70 words. Stack six of these in a row and you get a wall of matched blocks, each one readable on its own but monotonous as a set. This is the paragraph-level version of uniform sentence length, and it needs the same kind of deliberate variance.

## The shape bank

### 1. The one-line punch

A single sentence, standing alone as its own paragraph. Usually short, often the point of the whole section distilled to one line.

**Example:**

> Most people don't fail at this because they lack information. They fail because they never actually start.
>
> That's the whole problem.
>
> Everything else is detail.

**When to use it:** After a longer explanatory paragraph, to land the takeaway with nothing around it to dilute it. Do not overuse; a punch paragraph every other line stops punching.

### 2. The long unbroken paragraph followed by a fragment

One paragraph that runs longer than the surrounding ones, carrying real complexity or nuance, immediately followed by a very short paragraph (sometimes a fragment) that resets the pace.

**Example:**

> The reason most onboarding sequences fail isn't the content, it's the assumption baked into them: that a new user who just signed up is sitting there waiting to be taught, patient and attentive, ready to read five emails about features they haven't even encountered a need for yet, when in reality they are three seconds from closing the tab and forgetting your product exists by lunchtime.
>
> They're not waiting. They're leaving.

**When to use it:** When you want the long paragraph to do the work of building tension or complexity, then the short one to release it.

### 3. The list that interrupts prose

A short numbered or bulleted list breaks up a run of paragraph text, then prose resumes. This is a shape change, not just a formatting choice; it resets the reader's rhythm the same way a short sentence does.

**Example:**

> We tried three things before this worked.
>
> - Cutting the email sequence from seven to three.
> - Moving the demo earlier instead of gating it behind a form.
> - Killing the "book a call" CTA entirely for the first two weeks.
>
> Only the third one moved the number. The rest was noise we mistook for progress.

**When to use it:** Sparingly, when there genuinely are 2 to 4 parallel items. Do not use a list as a crutch for organizing thoughts that are not actually parallel; that reads as lazier than prose, not more human.

### 4. The mirrored two-sentence paragraph

Two short sentences of similar length, deliberately mirrored in structure, standing as their own paragraph. This is a rhetorical device, used sparingly, not a default shape.

**Example:**

> We didn't lose the client because of price. We lost the client because we stopped showing up.

**When to use it:** To land a contrast or a reveal. Overuse turns a rhetorical device into a tic; once or twice in a piece is plenty.

### 5. The medium paragraph with an embedded aside

A normal-length paragraph (3 to 4 sentences) with one sentence set off by a parenthetical or an em-dash-free aside using commas, which reads more like a spoken tangent than a written point.

**Example:**

> We rebuilt the whole intake form from scratch. It took longer than it should have, mostly because nobody wanted to be the one to admit the old version was theirs to begin with, and once it shipped, completions went up almost overnight. Not a subtle change. A visible one, the kind you can point to in a dashboard without squinting.

**When to use it:** As your default "workhorse" paragraph shape between punch paragraphs and lists, since it carries the most actual content while still varying internally.

### 6. The question paragraph

A single question, sometimes with a short follow-up, standing alone to shift the reader from being told something to being asked something.

**Example:**

> So why does this keep happening, even to teams who know better?
>
> Because knowing better and building differently are not the same skill.

**When to use it:** To transition between sections, or to re-engage a reader partway through a longer piece. Do not answer the question in the same paragraph; let the question land first.

## How to sequence shapes across a piece

Do not apply a random shape to every paragraph; that just creates a new, more elaborate uniform pattern. Instead, sequence deliberately:

1. Identify the 2 to 3 paragraphs carrying the most weight (the core argument or the key proof points). Give these the medium workhorse shape (#5), since they need room to build a real point.
2. Identify the single most important takeaway in the piece. Isolate it as a one-line punch (#1) or a mirrored two-sentence paragraph (#4), never both in the same piece unless it is a long piece with room for two distinct emphatic beats.
3. If there are 2 to 4 genuinely parallel items anywhere, break them into a list (#3) rather than prose. Do this at most once or twice per piece.
4. Use the long-then-fragment pattern (#2) at a natural tension point, usually right before a turn or a reveal.
5. Everywhere else, default to normal paragraphs of varying length (2 to 5 sentences), never letting two consecutive paragraphs land within about 15 words of total length of each other.

## Quick check

Read down the piece looking only at paragraph shape (ignore the words). If you can predict the next paragraph's approximate length and structure from the last two, the shape pattern needs more variance.
