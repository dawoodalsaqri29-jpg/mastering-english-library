# Platform Rewrite Gallery

The same core idea, adapted natively for email, LinkedIn, X, and blog, shown side by side. Use this to calibrate what "actually native" looks like versus a reformatted copy of the same draft. Notice that no two versions share an opening line, a length, or a closing move, even though the underlying idea is identical.

**Core idea (source content, not yet platform-adapted):** A solo attorney stopped taking referrals from her old firm and started offering flat-fee packages instead. It felt like a risk. It became the thing that let her actually control her schedule and her income, instead of taking whatever work showed up.

---

## Email version

Subject line: Why I stopped taking the easy referrals

"Quick story, because someone asked me this week how I decided to switch to flat-fee pricing.

For the first year on my own, I took every referral my old firm sent over. It was steady, and it was safe, and it was also completely out of my control: I never knew what a month would look like until the work showed up.

I turned down the biggest one I ever got. Told myself it was strategy. Really, it was closer to panic. I'd already committed to trying flat-fee packages with two clients that month and didn't have room for anything else.

That was the last referral I ever took from them. Everything since has been flat-fee, and for the first time, I know in January roughly what March looks like.

If you're a service provider still pricing by the hour and wondering whether a flat fee could work for what you do, happy to talk through how I structured mine. Just reply and I'll send you the breakdown.

Talk soon,
[NAME]"

**Why this is native to email:** direct address, a specific ask at the end (reply for the breakdown), a personal sign-off, and it references "someone asked me this week" the way a real message to one person does.

## LinkedIn version

"I turned down the biggest referral my old firm ever sent me.

Told myself it was strategy. It was actually panic: I'd already committed to two flat-fee clients that month and didn't have room for anything else.

That was the last referral I took from them. Everything since has been flat-fee work I priced myself, on my own terms.

A year in, the biggest difference isn't the income. It's that I know in January roughly what March looks like. Hourly billing never gave me that. I was always one slow month away from panic, no matter how good the last one had been.

If you're still pricing by the hour and wondering whether a flat fee could work for what you do: it's less risky than it feels, and the version of you that's scared to try it is the same version that took every referral out of fear instead of strategy."

**Why this is native to LinkedIn:** text-only, natural line breaks, no bold or checkmarks, a hook that survives truncation, no formal sign-off, ends on the point with a direct address to the reader instead of a call to comment.

## X version (single post)

"Turned down my biggest referral ever.

Told myself it was strategy.

It was panic, I just didn't have room for more hourly clients that month.

Best accident I ever made. Haven't taken an hourly client since."

**Why this is native to X:** under 280 characters, no hashtags, first-person and raw, ends on the sharpest line with no summary or CTA.

## Blog version

"It was a Tuesday afternoon when the referral call came in, the kind of referral I would have taken without a second thought a year earlier: a good client, steady work, exactly the kind of file my old firm used to hand me by default.

I said no.

Not because I'd found some grand strategic clarity about pricing models. I said no because I'd already committed to two flat-fee clients that month and physically didn't have room for anything else. It felt less like a decision and more like running out of hours at the worst possible time.

That accident turned out to be the last referral I ever took from my old firm. Everything since has been flat-fee, priced by me, for work I chose.

Here's what nobody tells you about switching off hourly billing: the scary part isn't the pricing model itself. It's the first month where you say no to easy, familiar work in favor of work you haven't proven you can fill yet. I didn't have a six-month runway or a business plan. I had two clients and a full calendar, and that was enough to force the question.

A year later, the biggest change isn't my income, though that's better too. It's that I know in January roughly what March is going to look like. Hourly billing never gave me that, no matter how good the previous month had been. I was always one slow stretch away from panic.

If you're still pricing by the hour and wondering whether flat-fee work could apply to what you do, start smaller than you think you need to. I didn't switch everything overnight. I just ran out of room to say yes to the old way, one Tuesday, and let that force the rest."

**Why this is native to a blog:** narrative opener (scene, not a definition), room to build the full arc with reflection in the middle, a clear single takeaway at the close instead of multiple competing CTAs, subheadings would actually hurt this piece since the narrative thread is the point.
